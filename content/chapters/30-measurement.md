## Chapter 30: How to know any of this is true

You are reading a table. Model names down the left side, hardware names across the top, tokens per second in the cells. It looks authoritative. Almost none of the numbers in it are comparable to each other, because each was produced by a different method, and the method is rarely printed.

Take a number from this book's own measurements. On 31 October 2026 a pair of NVIDIA DGX Spark machines running this book's engine produced 59.1 tokens per second of end-to-end generation throughput (prefill plus decode) at concurrency one. That figure came from four sequential requests of 400 tokens each, 1,600 tokens in 27.1 seconds of wall clock. Change the completion budget, the prompt length, the concurrency, or the cache state and the same machine gives you a different number. The measurement is real. On its own it is a rumour.

### Three instruments, three different answers

Three instruments produce these numbers, and each answers a different question.

Counters are the engine's own bookkeeping, exported in the Prometheus exposition format for scraping. They are monotonic sums since the process started, things like `prompt_tokens_total`, `request_prefill_seconds_sum`, and `mtp_accepted_total`. Dividing a sum by its count gives a mean, which is exactly how the 3.33 seconds mean prefill duration per request is computed. Divide `request_prefill_seconds_sum` by `_count` over 98 requests. Counters are cheap, they are always on, and they do not care what the client's network did. What they cannot give you is the shape of the distribution. A summary type that publishes only a sum and a count is a mean with extra steps. If the exporter publishes a histogram you get buckets, but the boundaries were chosen by whoever wrote the exporter, and anything finer than the narrowest bucket is gone. Counters also reset when the process restarts, so a rate computed across a restart is nonsense unless you detect the reset.

Logs are per-request records. They can give you the distribution, provided each line carries a start and end timestamp (or an explicit duration) alongside its token counts; a single completion timestamp plus token counts gives you throughput and inter-arrival times, not per-request latency. They fail in three ways. The timestamp is taken somewhere inside the server, not at the client, so the latency it records excludes network transit and client-side queueing — the number is lower than what the user actually experienced. (Clock skew between machines only corrupts latency if you subtract timestamps taken from different clocks.). Logs are sampled or rotated under load, so the requests you most want to see are the ones most likely to be missing. And logging costs time — synchronously in the request path, or asynchronously in CPU, memory, and I/O contention — so a heavily logged run can be slower than the run you were trying to measure, depending on how the logging is implemented and how much volume it carries

Wall-clock timing is what the user experiences. It includes tokenization, queueing for an admission slot, the scheduler's decision, prefill, decode, detokenization, and the client's own overhead. It is the only instrument that answers how long I waited, and it is the only one that cannot tell you why. When a request takes nearly twelve seconds, the wall clock says nearly twelve seconds. The counters say 3.33 seconds of prefill and 8.38 seconds of decode, and the gap between those two numbers and the wall clock is where queueing and overhead live. The mean time to first token over the same 98 requests was 3.46 seconds, so the mean queueing and other overhead before the first token was about 0.13 seconds.

### Units, averages, and what the mean hides

The first trap is a units problem that looks like an arithmetic problem. Mean decode duration over the 98 sampled requests is 8.38 seconds for 607 generated tokens, which is 72.4 tokens per second. The concurrency-one benchmark reported 59.1 tokens per second. Both get called decode throughput. They differ by more than twenty percent because the wall-clock figure divides 1,600 tokens by 27.1 seconds of total request time, and that total includes the prefill of each prompt, while the counter-based figure divides generated tokens by decode seconds alone. Neither is wrong. Quoting one without saying which is.

The second trap is the average itself. Ninety-eight requests is a small sample, and the mean of a bimodal distribution describes a request that never happened. This engine's prefix cache hit rate over the sampled window was 95.9 percent, 12,285,952 cached prompt tokens out of 12,804,579 looked up. A request that hits the cache skips the prefill matrix multiplies for its whole prefix and pays almost nothing. A request that misses pays for every token. The mean prefill duration of 3.33 seconds blends those two populations and describes neither. To compute a percentile you sort the 98 durations and use the nearest-rank method. For p99, the rank is ceil(0.99 × 98) = ceil(97.02) = 98, so p99 is the 98th value, which is the maximum. For p50, the rank is ceil(0.50 × 98) = 49. Report percentiles, and report the sample size next to them. A mean without a percentile is a claim about a population that may not exist.

### How the prefix cache contaminates prefill rates

The prefix cache is the confound that contaminates every prefill number in this book and most prefill numbers elsewhere. The mechanism is worth stating plainly. The serving stack splits the prompt into fixed-size token blocks. The block size is a configuration constant; this deployment's value is not in the verified metrics, so a reproduction should print it. For each block, the engine computes a hash over the token IDs in that block and the hash of the preceding block. The chained hash means a match at block k implies that every earlier block matches. A lookup starts at the root and walks the chain until a block hash is absent. The matched blocks are reused, and the first missing block and everything after it are recomputed. Collisions are handled by storing the full token block beside the hash and comparing token IDs before reuse, so a collision produces a miss rather than wrong key and value tensors. Eviction is by least-recently-used block under a reference count; a block shared by several sequences is not freed until the last sequence releases it. Partial trailing blocks are not cached, so a prompt whose length is not a multiple of the block size pays for the remainder.

The reused tokens still appear in `prompt_tokens_total`, because the counter counts tokens submitted, not tokens computed. Divide total prompt tokens by total prefill seconds and you are counting work that was skipped. Over the sampled window, `prompt_tokens_total` was 12,804,579 and the mean prefill duration was 3.33 seconds over 98 requests, so the total prefill time was 326.34 seconds. The apparent prefill rate is 12,804,579 / 326.34 = 39,237 tokens per second. The cache hit rate was 12,285,952 / 12,804,579 = 95.9 percent, so the uncached tokens were 518,627. The actual computed prefill rate was 518,627 / 326.34 = 1,589 tokens per second. The time saved by the cache is the cached tokens divided by that computed rate, 12,285,952 / 1,589 = 7,732 seconds. A cold run would take 326.34 + 7,732 = 8,058 seconds, so the cache removed about 95.9 percent of the modeled cold prefill compute time under the same per-token rate, not half; net wall-clock savings can be lower because cache lookup, retained blocks, and batch-capacity effects are not included. A tempting calculation divides cached tokens by the apparent rate and gets 313 seconds. That calculation is wrong because the apparent rate already includes the cached tokens. The cold-cache prefill is about 24.7 times the warm-cache prefill, not twice. The 39,237 figure is a legitimate measurement of a warm-cache workload, and it says nothing about raw prefill speed. Publishing it without the 95.9 percent hit rate invites every reader to draw the wrong conclusion.

### Warm-up costs that steady state never pays

Warm-up is the third trap. The first request after a process starts pays costs no later request pays. Four distinct costs can be separated with timestamps. CUDA graph capture records the decode loop's kernel launches into a graph; capture happens once per captured shape/batch size and costs a few iterations of the loop; new shapes or concurrency levels can trigger additional captures, so this cost can recur during warm-up. The memory allocator grows from its initial reservation to steady state as KV blocks and activation buffers are first touched. Kernel autotuning benchmarks candidate implementations for the shapes the first request presents. Weight paging covers any loader work deferred until first use. The engine can log a timestamp at process start, after graph capture, after allocator steady state, after autotune, and at first token. If each phase is independently instrumented and prefill is timestamped separately, the differences can attribute the penalty; otherwise overlapping phases and prefill inside the first-token interval make the deltas ambiguous. Without those timestamps you cannot say which share dominates. Do not average the first request into steady state. Report it separately, because a user who sends one request and waits experiences exactly that penalty and does not care about your steady state.

### Concurrency, rooflines, and the remaining confounds

Concurrency changes the answer by a factor no single number can express. At concurrency one this pair produced 59.1 tokens per second. At concurrency four it produced 106.2, a multiplier of 1.80, with the same four prompts fired concurrently and finishing in 15.1 seconds. The sub-linear multiplier is consistent with several causes, including a bandwidth ceiling, scheduler overhead, batching inefficiency, and KV cache growth. A roofline calculation can test whether bandwidth is the limiting factor; separating the remaining causes requires profiling or controlled experiments Let W be the bytes of model weights touched per decode step and K be the KV bytes read for the active sequences. The bandwidth ceiling on aggregate tokens per second is N·B/(W+K), where N is the number of active sequences, B is 273 GB/s, W is the bytes of model weights touched per decode step, and K is the KV bytes read for all active sequences in that step; at N = 1 this reduces to B/(W+K) At concurrency one, if the run were bandwidth-bound, the implied bytes per token would be 273/59.1 = 4.62 GB. At concurrency four, a perfect batched decode reads W once per step and produces four tokens. If the concurrency-one run was bandwidth-bound (so W is at most about 4.62 GB) and KV reads are small compared with W, the aggregate ceiling would approach 4 × 59.1 = 236.4 tokens per second; the observed 106.2 would then be 45 percent of that ceiling, implying scheduler, batching, or KV reads leave more than half the roofline unused. If W exceeds 4.62 GB, 236.4 is not a roofline and the shortfall cannot be attributed this way If the model's weight bytes are larger than 4.62 GB, the concurrency-one rate is not bandwidth-bound at all. The engine's `/metrics` endpoint reported 111,515,771,428 bytes of total unified memory on 2026-10-31, which includes weights, caches, and live streams; a full read of 111.5 GB at 273 GB/s would take 0.408 seconds and support only 2.45 tokens per second, so the observed rate cannot be explained by reading all resident bytes every token. Read W from the checkpoint metadata and K from the KV pool configuration before calling the multiplier a bandwidth signature. The per-stream rate at concurrency four is 26.6 tokens per second, which is what an individual user actually experiences. Quote 106.2 alone and you have promised a user 106 tokens per second and will deliver 26.6. Quote 59.1 alone and you have understated what the machine can do for a batch job by nearly a factor of two. Quote both, with the concurrency stated, and the reader can pick the one that matches their workload.

Four more confounds deserve a line each. The SM clock under load was observed at 2,197 MHz, from `nvidia-smi clocks.sm` at 2026-10-31 01:0x. NVIDIA's GB10 documentation lists a 3,003 MHz silicon maximum. The gap is consistent with a power limit, a current limit, or a thermal limit, and the clock metric alone does not identify which. Report the observed clock with the run conditions and the power and temperature telemetry if you want to claim a cause. The pair recorded 120,000 milliseconds of cumulative stall since start, from `tensorfold:pair_stall_ms` in `/metrics`. The metric is cumulative, so a single benchmark run must record its value at start and end and subtract. A wall-clock measurement includes stall only if the stall occurred inside the measured interval. Publishing the cumulative value without the definition and the delta is not evidence that a particular run was stalled. A memguard floor of 2.5 GiB kills a rank that drops below it, from `memguard.sh run .. 2.5 ..` in the live process list. A benchmark that silently lost a rank halfway through is not a benchmark. The speculative decoder accepted 67.6 percent of its drafted tokens, 42,181 out of 62,390. That acceptance rate is a diagnostic. It does not explain the decode number by itself. To attribute the decode number to speculation, run an ablation. Use the same four prompts, same concurrency, same cache state, and same completion budget. Run once with the draft head enabled and once with it disabled. Compare decode seconds to isolate the decode effect, but also report total wall clock and any draft-model load, prefill, and memory overhead; do not assume prefill is unchanged when enabling a separate draft model. Then run with a different draft model or a different acceptance threshold and report how acceptance and decode rate move together. Without that counterfactual, the 67.6 percent figure cannot explain 59.1 tokens per second; it is a covariate. A reader reproducing the run with a different draft model will not get the same tokens per second, and the report should say so.

### What a reproducible benchmark must state

A benchmark someone else can reproduce needs to state the model and its quantization, the hardware including memory type and bandwidth, the concurrency and the number of streams, the prompt length distribution and the completion budget, whether the prefix cache was cold, warm, or shared between runs, whether warm-up requests were discarded and how many, the clock observed under load, and the percentile table with its sample size. It should publish the raw counters alongside the derived rates, because the derived rates are where the errors live. It should say which instrument produced each number, because a wall-clock throughput and a counter-based throughput are different quantities that happen to share a unit. This deployment's KV pool is 262,144 positions shared across 4 streams, and the default reply budget is 32,768 tokens. Admission reserves `POOL/PARALLEL - MAX_TOKENS - ROUND_ROWS - 2`, which leaves a prompt-token admission ceiling of 65,536 - 32,768 - 16 - 2 = 32,750 tokens per stream (out of 65,536 total positions per stream). If the pool were raised to 1,048,576 positions, the per-stream ceiling would be 229,358 tokens and the extra KV memory would be 0.68 GiB per rank. Those numbers belong in a reproduction note because they determine whether a long prompt is admitted or rejected.

MLPerf, the benchmark suite launched in 2018 by an industry/academic consortium and now maintained by MLCommons, exists to enforce this. Its inference rules document, maintained in the MLCommons `inference_policies` repository, specifies warm-up, cache state, and allowable substitutions. The rules are long because the alternative is a table of numbers that cannot be compared, which is what most of the field publishes anyway.

When you read a tokens-per-second claim, ask what the cache hit rate was. Ask whether the figure is wall clock or counter-derived. Ask what concurrency it was measured at and what the per-stream rate was. Ask whether the first request was included. If the answer to any of those is not stated, you are reading a rumour with a decimal point.
