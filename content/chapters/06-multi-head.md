## Chapter 6: Why many small heads beat one large one

### The fixed budget of one softmax

One attention operation produces one probability distribution over the sequence, then one weighted average of value vectors. That weighted average is the entire output the layer writes. Every relation the layer wants to express has to pass through that single distribution, and the distribution has a fixed budget of mass summing to one.

Put numbers on it. Four positions carry scores 2.0, 1.0, 0.0 and −1.0. After exponentiation and normalization, e^2.0 = 7.389, e^1.0 = 2.718, e^0 = 1.000, e^−1.0 = 0.368, and the sum is 11.475, so the weights are 0.644, 0.237, 0.087 and 0.032. The output is 0.644 v_a + 0.237 v_b + 0.087 v_c + 0.032 v_d. The coefficients live inside the layer. What crosses the layer boundary is the sum, not the coefficients, so a downstream layer receives a single 512-vector and cannot tell which position contributed which part of it. The information about position is carried by the weights, and the weights are not written anywhere the next layer can read.

Now suppose two relations matter at once. To predict the token after "the cat sat on the", the model wants position i−1. To resolve "it" three clauses later, it wants the antecedent. A single head has to score both. Give both score 2.0 and the rest 0.0 and −1.0. The exponentials are 7.389, 7.389, 1.000, 0.368, summing to 16.146, so each relation gets 0.497. The output is 0.497 v_prev + 0.458 v_ant + 0.062 v_c + 0.023 v_d. Each signal arrives at less than half strength, and the two are added into one vector.

### Splitting attention into eight heads

Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar and the rest of the 2017 transformer team split that competition eight ways. Head h gets its own projections Q_h = X W_Q^h, K_h = X W_K^h, V_h = X W_V^h, each W a 512×64 matrix, so each head works in a 64-dimensional subspace. The score of token i against token j in head h is (q_i^h · k_j^h) / √64, and √64 = 8. The softmax runs over j inside head h alone. The head's output is o_i^h = Σ_j a_ij^h v_j^h, a 64-vector. The eight outputs concatenate into a 512-vector, and W_O, a 512×512 matrix, maps that concatenation into the residual stream. All eight heads read the same x_i, so they run in parallel and add no depth.

The parameter arithmetic shows what the split costs. Eight heads of width 64 use 8 × 512 × 64 = 262,144 weights for the query projection. One head of width 512 uses 512 × 512 = 262,144. The same holds for K, V and W_O, so the layer carries 4 × 262,144 = 1,048,576 attention weights either way. Splitting rearranges the numbers; it does not add or remove them.

### Rank expressiveness versus softmax scarcity

What changes is the number of softmaxes. A single 512-dimensional head can represent any bilinear query-key interaction of rank up to 512. A 64-dimensional head is capped at rank 64. Take B = I_512, the identity bilinear form. If B = U V^T with U and V both 512×64, then rank(B) ≤ min(rank U, rank V) ≤ 64, which contradicts rank(I_512) = 512. One wide head can express interactions that eight narrow heads cannot, in the bilinear sense. Raw expressiveness favors the wide head.

The wide head loses because the softmax is the scarce resource. Return to the two-relation sentence. Head 1 scores the previous token at 5.0 and the antecedent at 0.0. Head 2 scores the antecedent at 5.0 and the previous token at 0.0. With the other two positions at 0.0 and −1.0, head 1's exponentials are 148.4, 1.000, 1.000 and 0.368, summing to 150.8, so it puts 0.984 of its mass on the previous token. Head 2 does the same for the antecedent. Each signal now reaches the concatenation at 0.984 strength instead of 0.497, in its own 64-dimensional block.

### What a head writes versus where it looks

The concatenation is not the end of the story. W_O is dense, so head h's contribution to the residual is W_O[:, 64h : 64h+64] o^h, a 512-vector built from a 64-vector. Different heads write into different column subspaces of W_O, and those subspaces can overlap. A head's attention pattern is visible; its contribution to the residual is that pattern filtered through a learned 512×64 block. Saying "head 5 is a previous-token head" describes where head 5 looks, not what it writes.

### What heads learn and which ones matter

What heads learn has been mapped. Voita, Talbot, Moiseev, Sennrich and Titov (2019) trained a transformer on WMT English-Russian translation, put stochastic gates on the heads and penalised the number of gates that survived, and classified heads by their attention patterns into positional, syntactic and rare-word categories. They then pruned heads layer by layer and measured BLEU. Most encoder layers tolerated losing a large share of their heads with a small BLEU change, and the decoder was less prunable: their sparse model kept 14 of 48 encoder self-attention heads, but 31 of 48 decoder self-attention heads and 30 of 48 decoder-encoder heads. Clark, Khandelwal, Levy and Manning (2019) probed BERT-base by feeding a corpus through the model and clustering attention maps, finding heads that attend to the next token, to the [SEP] and [CLS] delimiters, to direct objects of verbs, and to coreferent mentions. Both papers point the same way: a minority of heads does most of the measurable work. Voita et al. showed that the rest can go

The cleanest circuit is the induction head, described by Olsson and colleagues at Anthropic in 2022. It needs two layers. A previous-token head in layer 1 attends from position i to i−1 and writes a copy of x_{i−1} into position i's residual. Call that copy c_i. In layer 2, a head's query is q_i = W_Q(x_i + c_i + r_i), where r_i collects the other residual components. The term W_Q c_i is a 64-vector produced by the 64×64 matrix W_Q W_O^{h1} acting on the layer-1 head's output. That product is the composition. On the key side, a parallel shift puts x_{j−1} into position j's key. The layer-2 query then matches keys at positions j where x_{j−1} = x_i, meaning j−1 is an earlier occurrence of the same token. Attention lands on j, and the value at j is x_j, the token that followed the earlier occurrence. The one-position offset between the match and the value is what turns "find the earlier occurrence" into "predict the next token."

Heads are not cleanly one thing. Elhage and colleagues at Anthropic (2022) studied toy models and showed that a network with more features to represent than dimensions to hold them will spread the features across near-orthogonal directions rather than drop them. A feature here is a direction in the residual stream; the model reads it by projecting onto that direction. In 512 dimensions, two features can be exactly orthogonal and interfere not at all. Three features in two dimensions cannot be mutually orthogonal; the best arrangement puts them 120° apart, and each pair has |cos θ| = 0.5, so reading one feature picks up half of another. Superposition buys capacity at the price of interference, and sparsity makes the price affordable, because features that rarely co-occur rarely interfere. The same pressure applies across heads. A head can act as a previous-token head on some inputs and as an induction component on others, and a probe returns an average of its behaviour.

Pruning separates two regimes. At inference, removing head h subtracts W_O[:, 64h:64h+64] o^h from the residual. If that term is small relative to the residual's norm, the layer's output barely moves, and Michel, Levy and Neubig (2019) found that many heads in a trained model can be removed this way with little task loss; they also found that some layers need nearly all their heads and others need only one, measured across checkpoints of a normally trained model rather than by retraining. Pruning during training is different in kind. The head's gradient path is gone from the first step, so the remaining heads must discover the layer's function without that route. Voita and colleagues measured this directly: a model trained with L0 head gates and then pruned beat retraining from scratch with the same small head count by 0.2 to 0.6 BLEU, which is the cost of removing the route before the layer has learned what it is for.

### Head count and the KV cache

The cost of many heads shows up in the KV cache. Per token per layer, the cache stores one key and one value vector of width d_model, so with 16-bit storage that is 2 × 512 × 2 = 2,048 bytes per token per layer, independent of how the 512 dimensions are divided among heads. Head count does not multiply the cache; d_model and layer count do. What multiplies is the number of independent key spaces. Multi-query attention (Shazeer, 2019) keeps the per-head queries but shares one key projection and one value projection across all heads, cutting the cache by a factor of eight in the eight-head case. Grouped-query attention (Ainslie et al. 2023) shares within groups, giving a factor of H/G. The independence lost is the ability of each head to score against its own key subspace. In MQA all heads read the same keys, so diversity has to come from the queries alone; in GQA only heads within a group share keys and values, so different groups still contribute distinct key/value subspaces and diversity can come from both the queries and the group-specific projections

On the engine this chapter draws from, the pool holds 262,144 positions shared across four streams. Each stream gets 65,536 positions before admission overhead, and the reply budget of 32,768 tokens plus 16 round-batch rows and 2 of bookkeeping leaves 32,750 positions per stream. Raising the pool to the model's native 1,048,576 positions costs 0.68 GiB per rank, which works out to about 928 bytes per added position per rank. That is the number to watch when head count and context length grow together.
