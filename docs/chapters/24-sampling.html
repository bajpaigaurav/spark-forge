Chapter 24 · VI. Producing a token

Temperature, top-k, top-p, and what each one does

Sampling parameters are not knobs on a single dial; each one cuts the distribution in a different place.

The final projection has run. Out of the residual stream comes a vector with one score per vocabulary entry, and now something has to turn that list into a single token. It is a few lines of arithmetic, and it decides, more than any other part of the serving stack, whether the output reads as prose or as a stutter.

Greedy decoding and the degeneration loop

The simplest rule is to take the argmax. Greedy decoding picks the highest-scoring token at every step, appends it, and runs the model again. It is deterministic, it is cheap, and it degenerates. Ari Holtzman and colleagues documented the failure in April 2019 in "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration", where maximising likelihood step by step produced text that looped, drifted, and contradicted itself. The mechanism is self-reinforcement. Suppose the context ends with "the the". The model has seen that pattern in training, so the next-token scores put "the" above "cat" and "dog". Greedy takes "the". The context becomes "the the the", an even stronger match for the repeated pattern, so the score for "the" rises again relative to the alternatives. The argmax locks the loop in. Nothing in the objective asks for variety, so nothing supplies it.

Temperature rescales logits without reordering

Temperature is the first fix, and it comes from statistical mechanics. Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski used it in Boltzmann machines in 1985. The operation is a division applied before the softmax. For logits zi, the probability of token i is pi = exp(zi/T) / ∑j exp(zj/T). Take three logits of 4.0, 2.0 and 1.0. At T = 1 the probabilities are 0.844, 0.114 and 0.042. At T = 0.5 the logits become 8, 4 and 2, and the probabilities become 0.980, 0.018 and 0.002. At T = 2 the logits become 2, 1 and 0.5, and the probabilities flatten to 0.628, 0.231 and 0.140. As T goes to zero the distribution collapses onto the argmax, so greedy decoding is the limit of temperature zero. As T goes to infinity it approaches the uniform distribution over the whole vocabulary. Temperature never reorders the candidates.

The logits 4.0, 2.0 and 1.0 stay in that order at every positive T, because division by a positive scalar and exponentiation both preserve order. What changes is the mass assigned to the tail, the lower-probability entries below the top few. High temperature gives the tail more mass; low temperature gives it less. Mathematically, no entry is removed at any positive T, so temperature has no explicit cutoff. In finite precision, however, a very small positive T can underflow tail exponentials to zero, effectively dropping those tokens; this is a numerical artifact rather than an explicit top-k cut At T=0 the division is undefined. Implementations special-case it as argmax, or clamp T to a small positive value; the limit is greedy, but the arithmetic needs the special case.

Count-based and mass-based truncation

Top-k sampling, introduced by Angela Fan, Mike Lewis and Yann Dauphin in their 2018 paper on hierarchical story generation, attacks that directly. Sort the distribution, keep the k highest entries, discard the rest, and renormalise what remains so it sums to one. With our three logits and k = 2, the kept mass is 0.844 + 0.114 = 0.958, and renormalisation gives 0.881 and 0.119. The third token is gone entirely. The weakness is that k is a count, and the right count depends on the shape of the distribution, which changes at every step. When the model is confident, one token holds almost all the mass, and k = 10 still admits nine tokens that are essentially impossible.

When the model is uncertain, fifty continuations might be plausible, and k = 10 throws away most of the probability mass and forces a choice among a fifth of the real options.

Holtzman's nucleus sampling, from the same 2019 paper, replaced the count with a cumulative target. Sort descending, add probabilities until the running total first reaches or exceeds p, keep that set, renormalise. With p = 0.9 and our three logits, the first token gives 0.844, which is short of 0.9, so the second is admitted and the total reaches 0.958. Two tokens survive, and the result is identical to top-k with k = 2. The difference shows up elsewhere. Ten equally likely tokens at 0.1 each: top-p at 0.9 keeps nine of them, while top-k at 5 keeps five and discards half the mass. One token at 0.95 with the rest spread thin: top-p keeps exactly one, while top-k at 50 keeps fifty entries, forty-nine of which are noise.

The truncation width now adapts to the shape of the distribution rather than a fixed count; it is not determined by pmax alone—two distributions with the same top-token probability can yield different nucleus sizes The cost is a sort over the vocabulary, which is why implementations use partial selection or a threshold scan rather than a full ordering.

Min-p sets a floor relative to the top token

Min-p, described by Minh Nguyen and colleagues in their 2024 preprint "Min-p sampling", takes a third approach. Instead of a cumulative target, it sets a floor relative to the top token: keep everything whose probability is at least min_p times the maximum. With pmax = 0.844 and min_p = 0.1, the threshold is 0.0844, so the 0.844 and the 0.114 survive and the 0.042 does not. After thresholding, divide the surviving probabilities by their sum. Here the sum is 0.958, so the renormalised probabilities are 0.881 and 0.119. When the model is confident and pmax = 0.99, the threshold becomes 0.099 and only the top token survives. When the model is genuinely uncertain and pmax = 0.12, the threshold drops to 0.012 and many more tokens survive than in the confident case, though at production vocabulary sizes the 1.2% floor still discards most of the long tail.

The cutoff scales with confidence automatically, without a fixed count and without a cumulative target that can slice through a flat distribution at an arbitrary point. It does have a subtlety. Temperature rescales the logits before softmax, so pmax changes nonlinearly and the effective floor moves when you change T At T=1 with our three logits, pmax=0.844 and min_p=0.1 gives a threshold of 0.0844, keeping two tokens. At T=0.5, pmax=0.980 and the threshold becomes 0.098, so only the top token survives. At T=2, pmax=0.628 and the threshold becomes 0.0628, so all three tokens survive. People who tune the two independently often end up with a sampler that behaves differently from what they intended.

Penalties and the order of operations

Penalties work on a different surface. The repetition penalty from Keskar and colleagues' CTRL paper in September 2019 walks the logits for tokens already present in the context and divides the positive ones by a factor r while multiplying the negative ones by r. For a token with positive logit, dividing by r&gt;1 lowers it; for a negative logit, multiplying by r&gt;1 makes it more negative. The map is monotone on each side of zero, so two repeated tokens keep their relative order. A repeated token can fall below a non-repeated token, which is the point. The factor r is a scalar greater than 1; values below 1 would reward repetition. Frequency penalties subtract an amount α proportional to how many times a token has already appeared; presence penalties subtract a flat amount β the first time. The scope matters.

Repetition penalty applies to tokens already in the context, and implementations differ on whether the prompt counts. Many apply penalties only to generated tokens so that prompt words are not suppressed. All of these act on logits, before the softmax, so they reshape the entire distribution rather than trimming its tail. A worked example with logits 4.0, 2.0 and 1.0 and a repetition penalty r=1.2 on the first token gives logits 3.333, 2.0 and 1.0. The probabilities become 0.735, 0.194 and 0.071: the first falls from 0.844, while the other two rise from 0.114 and 0.042 as the distribution renormalizes A frequency penalty with α=0.5 and count 2 on the first token gives logits 3.0, 2.0 and 1.0, and probabilities 0.665, 0.245 and 0.090. A presence penalty with β=0.5 gives logits 3.5, 2.0 and 1.0, and probabilities 0.766, 0.171 and 0.063.

Order matters when you stack them. Penalties first, then temperature, then truncation. The reason is that mass-based truncation such as top-p/min-p is a function of the final probabilities; top-k is rank-based and temperature-invariant, so its selected set is unchanged by temperature Take logits 4.0, 2.0 and 1.0 and a repetition penalty r=2 on the first token. Penalties first give logits 2.0, 2.0 and 1.0 and probabilities 0.422, 0.422 and 0.155. Top-p at 0.9 now keeps all three, because the cumulative reaches 0.999. Truncate first on the unpenalised distribution and top-p at 0.9 keeps only the first two, excluding the third token that the penalty made competitive. Temperature belongs between penalties and truncation because it changes the probabilities that the cumulative target or the min-p floor reads. If you truncate before temperature, you choose a set using the wrong mass.

After truncation and renormalisation, the token is drawn from the remaining categorical distribution. The draw is random; argmax is a different decoder. A sampler with T=1 and no truncation samples the full softmax.

Beam search and length normalisation

Beam search keeps B hypotheses alive at once, expands each, and retains the B highest-scoring partial sequences by cumulative log-probability. The raw score of a hypothesis Y is ∑t log p(yt | y&lt;t). Length normalisation, introduced for neural machine translation by Wu and colleagues in 2016, divides that score by a length penalty. In the GNMT formulation, lp(Y) = ((5+|Y|)alpha)/(6alpha), with α=0.6, and the beam score is the log-probability divided by lp(Y). Take two hypotheses. H1 has length 2 and log-probability -1.2. H2 has length 4 and log-probability -1.3. Raw scores favour H1, because -1.2 is greater than -1.3. With length normalisation, lp(H1)=70.6/60.6=1.097, so H1 scores -1.094. lp(H2)=90.6/60.6=1.275, so H2 scores -1.020. The longer hypothesis wins. When a beam emits an end-of-sequence token, that hypothesis is completed and removed from the active set; its score is stored.

The search stops when B hypotheses are complete or the length cap is reached, and length normalisation is applied at completion. Beam search remained the default in statistical machine translation through Koehn's Moses in 2007 and in neural machine translation systems such as Wu et al.'s 2016 system.

It is rarely used for open-ended generation, and the reasons are mechanical as well as qualitative. Maximising likelihood selects the mode of the sequence distribution, and Holtzman et al. showed that mode-seeking decoding tends to loop and drift, so beam search inherits the failure that greedy decoding has. The cost is also real. Continuous batching lets the scheduler add and remove requests at each decode step. Each request carries its own KV cache, and the batch is assembled from whatever requests are active. A beam search request is one request with several hypotheses, each needing its own KV cache and its own next-token computation. With B=4 and the live pool of 262,144 positions split across four streams, the equal share is 65,536 positions. A single request's admission ceiling is 32,750 tokens after reserving the 32,768-token reply budget and overhead.

Each beam also needs its own reply budget, so four beams at that ceiling would need about 262,000 positions—the whole pool—not 131,000 So beam search either shrinks the context per beam or blocks admission. Beams also finish at different times. A beam that emits end-of-sequence is complete, but the other beams continue; the request cannot be retired until the search finishes, so the scheduler holds its KV cache and its slot while other requests wait or share the batch.

The sampler cannot add information to the distribution it is given. It can only decide how much of the model's uncertainty reaches the categorical draw, and each parameter above makes that decision at a different point in the arithmetic. When you tune, change one parameter at a time and re-run the same prompt. A change in T can move the min-p floor, and a change in penalties can move the truncation set.
