Chapter 30 · VIII. Grounding it

How to know any of this is true

A performance claim without a methodology is a rumour, and most published numbers are not comparable to each other.

You are reading a table. Model names down the left side, hardware names across the top, tokens per second in the cells. It looks authoritative. Almost none of the numbers in it are comparable to each other, because each was produced by a different method, and the method is rarely printed.

Take a single card serving a 70B model at four bits. One report lands near the memory-bound ceiling for batch one. Another reports two thirds of that, from a mixed workload. Both are honest. The first divides the tokens the model generated by the seconds the decode loop ran, on a batch of one, with the KV cache already warm. The second divides every token the client received by the wall-clock seconds the client waited, across a mixed workload with prompts arriving during the run. Change the completion budget, the prompt length, the concurrency, or the cache state and the same card gives you a different number. Each measurement is real. On its own it is a rumour.

Three instruments, three different answers

Three instruments produce these numbers, and each answers a different question.

Counters are the engine's own bookkeeping, exported for scraping. They are monotonic sums since the process started, things like prompt tokens submitted, seconds spent in prefill, and requests completed. Dividing a sum by its count gives a mean. Counters are cheap, they are always on, and they do not care what the client's network did. What they cannot give you is the shape of the distribution. A summary type that publishes only a sum and a count is a mean with extra steps. If the exporter publishes a histogram you get buckets, but the boundaries were chosen by whoever wrote the exporter, and anything finer than the narrowest bucket is gone. Counters also reset when the process restarts, so a rate computed across a restart is nonsense unless you detect the reset.

Logs are per-request records. They can give you the distribution, provided each line carries a start and an end timestamp, or an explicit duration, alongside its token counts; a single completion timestamp plus token counts gives you throughput and inter-arrival times, not per-request latency. They fail in three ways. The timestamp is taken somewhere inside the server, not at the client, so the latency it records excludes network transit and client-side queueing, and the number is lower than what the user experienced. Logs are sampled or rotated under load, so the requests you most want to see are the ones most likely to be missing. And logging costs time, synchronously in the request path or asynchronously in CPU, memory and I/O contention, so a heavily logged run can be slower than the run you were trying to measure.

Wall-clock timing is what the user experiences. It includes tokenization, queueing for an admission slot, the scheduler's decision, prefill, decode, detokenization, and the client's own overhead. It is the only instrument that answers how long I waited, and it is the only one that cannot tell you why. When a request takes eleven seconds, the wall clock says eleven seconds. The counters say eight seconds of prefill and two of decode, and the gap between the sum and the wall clock is where queueing and overhead live.

Units, averages, and what the mean hides

The first trap is a units problem that looks like an arithmetic problem. Decode throughput and end-to-end throughput share a unit and measure different things. A 70B model at four bits reads 35 GB of weights per step, so on a card with 3,350 GB/s the memory-bound step floor is 10.4 ms, which is 3,350 ÷ 35 = 95.7 tokens per second at batch one. Divide generated tokens by decode seconds and you can approach that ceiling. Divide every token in the request, prompt included, by the total request seconds and you are averaging the prefill in, so the same run reports less. Neither is wrong. Quoting one without saying which is.

The second trap is the average itself. A mean of a bimodal distribution describes a request that never happened. Serving traffic is routinely bimodal, because a request that hits the prefix cache skips the prefill matrix multiplies for its whole prefix and pays almost nothing, while a request that misses pays for every token. Average the two populations and you get a prefill duration that describes neither. To compute a percentile you sort the durations and use the nearest-rank method. For p99 with 100 requests the rank is ceil(0.99 × 100) = 100, so p99 is the maximum. For p50 the rank is ceil(0.50 × 100) = 50. Report percentiles, and report the sample size next to them. A mean without a percentile is a claim about a population that may not exist.

How the prefix cache contaminates prefill rates

The prefix cache is the confound that contaminates most published prefill numbers. The mechanism is worth stating plainly. The serving stack splits the prompt into fixed-size token blocks. For each block, the engine computes a hash over the token IDs in that block and the hash of the preceding block. The chained hash means a match at block k implies that every earlier block matches. A lookup walks the chain until a block hash is absent. The matched blocks are reused, and the first missing block and everything after it are recomputed. Collisions are handled by storing the full token block beside the hash and comparing token IDs before reuse, so a collision produces a miss rather than wrong key and value tensors. Eviction is by least-recently-used block under a reference count; a block shared by several sequences is not freed until the last sequence releases it.

Partial trailing blocks are not cached, so a prompt whose length is not a multiple of the block size pays for the remainder.

The reused tokens still count as submitted tokens, because the counter counts tokens accepted, not tokens computed. Divide submitted tokens by prefill seconds and you are counting work that was skipped. Suppose a 65,536-token prompt arrives against a warm cache holding the first 32,750 tokens. Only 65,536 − 32,750 = 32,786 tokens are computed. The apparent rate, submitted tokens over prefill time, is therefore 65,536 ÷ 32,786 ≈ 2 times the computed rate, because the same seconds bought half the work. The apparent rate is a legitimate measurement of a warm-cache workload, and it says nothing about raw prefill speed. Publishing it without the hit ratio invites every reader to draw the wrong conclusion. Report both: tokens submitted, tokens computed, and the seconds they took.

Prefix caching changes the prefill budget rather than the decode budget, and its economics are simple once the ratio is explicit. A block that hits costs a hash lookup and a reference-count bump. A block that misses costs a full pass through every layer for every token in the block. A miss at any block invalidates the rest of the chain, so the hit rate depends on how much prompt structure the workload shares. System prompts, tool schemas and few-shot prefixes are the usual source of sharing; independent user questions are not.

Warm-up costs that steady state never pays

Warm-up is the third trap. The first request after a process starts pays costs no later request pays. Four distinct costs can be separated with timestamps. CUDA graph capture records the decode loop's kernel launches into a graph; capture happens once per captured shape and batch size and costs a few iterations of the loop, and new shapes or concurrency levels can trigger additional captures. The memory allocator grows from its initial reservation to steady state as KV blocks and activation buffers are first touched. Kernel autotuning benchmarks candidate implementations for the shapes the first request presents. Weight paging covers any loader work deferred until first use. The engine can log a timestamp at process start, after graph capture, after allocator steady state, after autotune, and at first token.

If each phase is independently instrumented and prefill is timestamped separately, the differences can attribute the penalty; otherwise overlapping phases and prefill inside the first-token interval make the deltas ambiguous. Without those timestamps you cannot say which share dominates. Do not average the first request into steady state. Report it separately, because a user who sends one request and waits experiences exactly that penalty and does not care about your steady state.

Concurrency, rooflines, and the remaining confounds

Concurrency changes the answer by a factor no single number can express, and the roofline is the tool for reasoning about it. Let W be the bytes of model weights touched per decode step, K the KV bytes read for all active sequences in that step, B the bandwidth, and N the number of active sequences. One pass over the weights serves every row, so the step moves W once and produces N tokens, and the aggregate ceiling is N × B ÷ (W + K).

Work it on the same card. A 70B model at four bits gives W = 35 GB. For the cache term, take Llama 2 70B with 80 layers, 8 KV heads and a head dimension of 128 at two bytes per value. Each layer holds 8 × 128 = 1,024 values per position; keys and values across all layers come to 2 × 80 × 1,024 = 163,840 values per position, and at two bytes each that is 163,840 × 2 = 327,680 bytes. At a 100,000-token context one stream's cache is 100,000 × 327,680 = 32,768,000,000 bytes, comparable to the weight read. Four streams still read the weights once but re-read the cache per stream, so the cache term is 4 × 32,768,000,000 = 131,072,000,000 bytes and dominates the step.

Per-stream throughput falls even as aggregate throughput rises, and the crossover from bandwidth-bound to compute-bound arrives sooner at long context.

That is why quoting a single rate is misleading in both directions. Quote the batch-one rate and you have understated what the card can do for a batch job. Quote the aggregate at concurrency four and you have promised a user a rate they will not see, because each stream gets a quarter of it. Quote both, with the concurrency and the context length stated, and the reader can pick the one that matches their workload.

Four more confounds deserve a line each. Clock throttling: an SM clock observed under load is not the silicon maximum, and the gap is consistent with a power limit, a current limit, or a thermal limit, and the clock alone does not identify which. Report the observed clock with the run conditions and the power and temperature telemetry if you want to claim a cause. Cumulative counters: a stall or error counter that accumulates since process start must be read at the start and end of a run and subtracted, and publishing the cumulative value without the delta is not evidence that a particular run was affected. Health events: a run in which a process was killed and restarted halfway through is not a run, so state the watchdog, the memory floor, and whether any restart occurred.

Feature attribution: an acceptance rate, a cache hit rate or a kernel occupancy is a covariate, not an explanation. To attribute a throughput number to a feature, run an ablation with the same prompts, the same concurrency, the same cache state and the same completion budget, and toggle only that feature.

What a reproducible benchmark must state

A benchmark someone else can reproduce needs to state the model and its quantization, the hardware including memory type and bandwidth, the concurrency and the number of streams, the prompt length distribution and the completion budget, whether the prefix cache was cold, warm, or shared between runs, whether warm-up requests were discarded and how many, the clock observed under load, and the percentile table with its sample size. It should publish the raw counters alongside the derived rates, because the derived rates are where the errors live. It should say which instrument produced each number, because a wall-clock throughput and a counter-based throughput are different quantities that happen to share a unit.

Capacity belongs in that note too, because it decides whether a long prompt is admitted at all. A pool of 262,144 positions split across four streams gives 65,536 positions per stream. Admission subtracts a 32,768-token reply budget, 16 rows for the decode round batch, and 2 positions for boundary slack: 65,536 − 32,768 − 16 − 2 = 32,750 tokens per stream. Raise the pool to 1,048,576 positions and the same arithmetic gives 1,048,576 ÷ 4 = 262,144, minus 32,768, minus 16, minus 2, for 229,358 tokens. A benchmark run at a context length above the first figure is measuring a different admission regime, and the report should say so.

MLPerf, the benchmark suite launched in 2018 by an industry and academic consortium and now maintained by MLCommons, exists to enforce this. Its inference rules, maintained in the MLCommons inference_policies repository, specify warm-up, cache state and allowable substitutions. The rules are long because the alternative is a table of numbers that cannot be compared, which is what most of the field publishes anyway.

When you read a tokens-per-second claim, ask what the cache hit rate was. Ask whether the figure is wall clock or counter-derived. Ask what concurrency it was measured at and what the per-stream rate was. Ask whether the first request was included. If the answer to any of those is not stated, you are reading a rumour with a decimal point.
