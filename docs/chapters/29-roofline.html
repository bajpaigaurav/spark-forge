What the hardware actually allows

You can predict a model's tokens per second from its size and the memory bandwidth before you ever run it.

Ask someone who runs inference for a living how many tokens per second a given box will produce and they will usually answer before they run anything. The arithmetic is short. It needs two numbers, the bytes the machine can move per second and the bytes the model must move to emit one token.

The roofline model and decode's intensity

The formal version is the roofline model, published by Samuel Williams, Andrew Waterman and David Patterson in 2009 in Communications of the ACM. Attainable performance is the minimum of peak floating-point throughput and memory bandwidth multiplied by arithmetic intensity, the FLOPs performed per byte moved. Plot performance against intensity on log axes and you get a rising line that meets a flat ceiling. The flat part is the compute roof, the rising part the bandwidth roof. Where a workload lands tells you what to tune.

Decode lands far to the left. To emit one token, the model runs the new token's hidden state through every layer in sequence. At each layer the engine streams that layer's weight matrix out of memory, through L2, into the SMs, multiplies, and discards the tile before moving to the next layer. Nothing survives from one token to the next, because the matrices are far larger than any cache on the device. A 70-billion-parameter model in FP16 occupies 140 GB, and no single accelerator caches 140 GB. Bytes read per token are therefore approximately the active parameter count times bytes per parameter, and the arithmetic is about one multiply-add per parameter, or 2 FLOPs per 2 bytes at FP16. Intensity near 1.

Put that against the hardware. An H100 SXM has 989 TFLOPS of dense FP16 tensor throughput and 3,350 GB/s of HBM3 bandwidth, a ratio of 295 FLOPs per byte. That ratio is the ridge point. A workload at intensity 1 sits three hundred times below it, so decode is bandwidth-bound by a wide margin, and the compute units spend most of the step waiting on memory.

What batching amortizes

Batching changes the read without changing the arithmetic. When b requests decode together, the engine still streams each weight tile once, but it multiplies that tile by b rows before discarding it. The read is amortized b ways; the FLOPs are not. The bytes moved per decode step, for the whole batch, has four terms

B_step = P_active × bytes_per_param + b × KV_bytes(L) + b × workspace(L) + TP_exchange(b)

where P_active is the parameter count the batch actually touches, L is context length, and TP_exchange is tensor-parallel all-reduce traffic. For a dense model P_active is the whole parameter count. For a mixture-of-experts model it is the shared layers plus whichever experts the router selects, and with a batch of b tokens the union of selected experts grows, so the weight term rises with b even though each token touches only a fraction of the weights.

The KV term needs its own derivation. Every layer stores the key and value vectors for every position already generated, so attention does not recompute them. The layout is per layer, per KV head, per position, per head dimension. Bytes per token of context is

KV_bytes = 2 × n_layers × n_kv_heads × d_head × dtype_bytes

where the 2 covers K and V, and a sequence of L positions at batch b multiplies the result by L × b. Work it for Llama 2 70B, which has 80 layers, 8 KV heads and a head dimension of 128, stored at two bytes. Each layer holds 8 × 128 = 1,024 values per position. Keys and values across all layers come to 2 × 80 × 1,024 = 163,840 values per position, which at two bytes each is 163,840 × 2 = 327,680 bytes. At a 100,000-token context that one stream's cache is 100,000 × 327,680 = 32,768,000,000 bytes, which is nearly the size of the model's four-bit weight footprint.

The arithmetic behind a bandwidth number

Now a worked budget on a single H100. The card lists 192 GB of HBM3 and 3,350 GB/s. A 70B model at FP16 stores 140 GB of weights, approximately the bytes read per decoded token, and its bandwidth-bound ceiling is 3,350 ÷ 140 = 23.9 tokens per second. At four bits the same model needs 35 GB, and the ceiling is 3,350 ÷ 35 = 95.7 tokens per second. A 7B model at FP16 needs 14 GB; the ceiling is 3,350 ÷ 14 = 239 tokens per second. At four bits that model needs 14 ÷ 4 = 3.5 GB. The ceiling is then 3,350 ÷ 3.5 = 957 tokens per second. GPT-3, described by Brown and colleagues in 2020 at 175 billion parameters, would need 350 GB per token at FP16.

Its ceiling would be 3,350 ÷ 350 = 9.57 tokens per second, and it would not fit on the card at that precision.

Those ceilings are upper bounds, not predictions. They assume the kernel reaches peak bandwidth, which it does not; real decode on a large model typically lands at half to two thirds of the roof. They assume nothing else is moving bytes, and the KV cache, the workspace and the collectives all share the same bus. They also assume batch one, where the weight read dominates. Raise the batch and the roof itself moves.

Quantization as a bandwidth multiplier

Quantization is usually sold on the wrong grounds. It is pitched as a way to fit a model into memory; its larger effect on serving is proportional speed. Halving bytes per parameter doubles the bandwidth-bound ceiling, provided the kernel still reaches the same fraction of peak bandwidth and the arithmetic does not become the new constraint. It is not exact, because dequantization costs instructions, group scales cost bytes, and kernel efficiency at 4 bits is rarely the same as at 16.

Group-wise quantization, as in GPTQ (Frantar and colleagues, 2022) and AWQ (Lin and colleagues, 2023), stores a scale per group of weights rather than per tensor. Both papers motivate this with outlier channels that dominate a per-tensor scale and leave the rest of the matrix with a coarse step size; smaller groups confine the damage. A group size of 128 with FP16 scales adds 2 bytes per 128 weights. Against a half-byte four-bit weight that is a few percent of the stream, so the 70B ceiling drops slightly from 95.7 tokens per second rather than falling off a cliff.

The same reasoning explains why 8-bit weights buy less than the label suggests. Going from FP16 to 8 bits halves the read and doubles the ceiling, and going to 4 bits halves it again. But the arithmetic intensity doubles with each step too, so a workload that was comfortably bandwidth-bound at 16 bits can approach the ridge at 4 bits if the batch is large. At some batch size the compute roof takes over, and further quantization stops helping.

What real decode reveals about the step budget

The roof inverts into a diagnostic. If a model emits X tokens per second at batch one, then bytes per token is at most bandwidth ÷ X, because time is at least bytes divided by bandwidth. The bound holds without knowing which model or precision is loaded, which is why it is worth stating when the model card is not in front of you. Run the inversion at the roof. A 70B model at four bits tops out at 95.7 tokens per second on an H100, and at that rate the implied read is 3,350 ÷ 95.7 = 35 GB per token, exactly the weight footprint. Any measured rate below the roof implies a larger read, which is the KV cache and the workspace showing up on the same bus.

The same rate on a card with 1,000 GB/s would imply a read of at most 1,000 ÷ 95.7 = 10.4 GB, less than the weights themselves, so the roof there is lower and the same rate is not achievable.

The gap opens when you batch. If decode were purely weight-bandwidth-bound, four streams should give close to four times the aggregate, because one pass over the matrices serves all four rows. Nothing like that happens once the cache dominates. Take the same 70B model at four bits and a 100,000-token context on four streams. The weight term stays at 35 GB per step, because one read serves all rows, but the KV term quadruples: 4 × 32,768,000,000 = 131,072,000,000 bytes. The KV term quadruples: 3 × 32,768,000,000 = 98,304,000,000 extra bytes per step, on top of the 35 GB weight read. Per-stream throughput falls even as aggregate throughput rises, and long context changes which term sets the step.

That is the asymmetry worth carrying away. Batching amortizes the weight term, which is fixed per step, and multiplies the KV term, which scales with batch and context. Attention arithmetic grows the same way: per token per layer, the score and value products cost 4 × n_heads × d_head × L FLOPs, linear in context length and not shared across streams. The weight read does not grow with L at all. So the crossover from bandwidth-bound to compute-bound arrives sooner at long context and large batch, and the roofline point slides right.

Why prefill, speculation, and capacity set different limits

Prefill behaves completely differently, and the contrast is the clearest illustration of what the roofline is for. Prefill processes the whole prompt at once, so the weight read is shared across all T prompt tokens. The linear-layer term gives roughly T FLOPs per byte, since each weight is used T times and costs two bytes at FP16, but attention adds about 4 × n_layers × h × T FLOPs per token, so the true intensity carries a term quadratic in T. At a 100,000-token prompt the linear term alone is 100,000 FLOPs per byte, more than three hundred times the H100's ridge point of 295 FLOPs per byte. Prefill is compute-bound on that part while a decode step at intensity 1 is bandwidth-bound, and the two phases want different optimizations for that reason.

Speculative decoding bends the decode budget in the other direction. A small draft model proposes k tokens, the main model verifies them in one pass, and the accepted prefix plus one bonus token is emitted. If each draft is accepted with probability a, the expected yield from a step that drafts k tokens is 1 + a + a² + … + a^k, which converges to 1/(1 − a). At a = 0.8 the limit is five tokens per weight read; at a = 0.5 it is two. No draft length pushes the yield past the limit, so effective bytes per token is at least the weight footprint divided by that yield. The draft model's own reads and the verification overhead eat into the gain, which is why the realized speedup falls short of the bound.

Capacity then constrains context directly, and the arithmetic is the same admission rule the serving chapters use. A KV pool of 262,144 positions shared across four streams gives each stream 65,536 positions. Admission subtracts the 32,768-token default reply budget, 16 rows for the decode round batch, and 2 positions for boundary overhead, which gives 65,536 − 32,768 − 16 − 2 = 32,750 tokens per stream. Raising the pool to the model's native 1,048,576 positions lifts the ceiling to 1,048,576 ÷ 4 = 262,144, minus 32,768, minus 16, minus 2, for 229,358 tokens per stream, at four times the cache cost. On a 192 GB card that fits beside a 70B model at four bits; on an 80 GB card it does not fit beside the same model at eight bits.

What follows is a view about hardware. For single-stream decode the number that matters is bandwidth, and the capacity number decides whether the model fits at all. A card with 3,350 GB/s and 192 GB can hold a 70B model at four bits with room for a large KV pool, and its decode roof is 95.7 tokens per second. A card with 80 GB can hold the same model at eight bits and little else, and its roof is half as high. The two limits bind at different points, and quoting one without the other describes a benchmark rather than a deployment.
