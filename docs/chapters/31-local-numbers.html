Two GB10 boxes sit on a desk with a cable between them, and the model they serve is sharded across the pair: the aggregate footprint is 111.5 GB, so each rank holds roughly 55.8 GB. The question worth asking is whether the previous thirty chapters let you say what those boxes will do before you run anything. Prediction first, measurement second, and the places where the two disagree are the only places worth writing down.

The shared memory budget and the memguard kill switch

The engine's footprint across the pair is 111.5 GB, or 111,515,771,428 bytes, covering weights, KV caches and live streams together. On this hardware there is no separate VRAM to spill into. The GPU and the CPU draw from the same LPDDR5X, and the whole of it moves at 273 GB/s according to NVIDIA's DGX Spark hardware guide. Every byte of cache you allocate is a byte the host page cache cannot have. That is why the software carries a guard. memguard.sh reads MemAvailable from /proc/meminfo on a timer. If the reading falls below 2.5 GiB, it sends SIGKILL to the rank PID. SIGKILL cannot be caught, so the rank does not flush caches or close sockets cleanly. The kernel closes the sockets, in-flight requests get a connection reset, and the launcher restarts the rank. The model reloads its shard from disk, and the client retries the failed request.

Unified memory is what makes a model this large fit on a desk, and it is also what collapses the memory budget into a single shared number instead of two.

Tensor parallelism and the all-reduce stall

The model is split across the two ranks layer by layer, so each rank holds a shard of every weight matrix and the two have to agree on the activations at each boundary. The primitive is an all-reduce. Each transformer layer has two boundaries that cross the split, one after attention and one after the MLP, so a model with L layers needs 2L all-reduces per forward pass. The payload is the batch activation matrix in bf16. At decode the batch is at most 16 rows, so the payload is at most 16 × H × 2 bytes per boundary, where H is the hidden width. At prefill the same matrix has 130,659 rows, so the prefill payload is 130,659/16 = 8,166 times larger. The all-reduce is issued after the local matmul and before the next layer's matmul.

It can overlap with the next layer's compute only if the scheduler has independent work, which at decode it usually does not. When one rank falls behind, the other waits inside the collective doing nothing. The pair has accumulated 120,000 ms of stall since start, recorded by tensorfold:pair_stall_ms. That counter is cumulative and not decomposed by collective or rank, so it cannot support a per-layer latency claim or an upper-bound claim. It is a separate term you have to measure.

Decode throughput and concurrency scaling

Start with decode, where the roofline chapter made its sharpest claim. That chapter said tokens per second is memory bandwidth divided by the bytes the engine touches per token. One stream on this pair produced 59.1 tok/s. The probe was four prompts of 400 completion tokens each, 1,600 tokens in total, run one after another in 27.1 s, and 1,600 divided by 27.1 is about 59.0. The roofline formula is tok/s = 273 GB/s ÷ bytes per token. If I plug in the measured 59.1 tok/s, the implied bytes per token is 273 / 59.1 = 4.62 GB. Plug that back in and 273 / 4.62 = 59.1. That is the instantiated formula.

The 4.62 GB is the effective per-token byte rate implied by throughput — it folds in all-reduce stall and other non-memory time, so it is not a measured DRAM read The 111.5 GB total footprint includes caches and live streams that are not read once per token.

The same four prompts fired concurrently finished in 15.1 s, and 1,600 over 15.1 is 106.2 tok/s. The aggregate multiplier is 106.2 divided by 59.1, which comes to 1.80x. Under a pure bandwidth bound, batching should be close to free, because the weights are read once per step and reused across every row in the batch. Four streams ought to approach four times the single-stream rate before compute becomes the limit. The linear prediction is 4 × 59.1 = 236.4 tok/s. The measured 106.2 tok/s is 106.2/236.4 = 45% of that. Per stream, 106.2/4 = 26.55 tok/s, which is 26.55/59.1 = 45% of the single-stream rate. Getting 1.80x says the crossover arrived earlier than the model suggested. Two candidate explanations are clock and kernel width, but this run does not isolate them. Under load the SMs run at 2,197 MHz according to nvidia-smi clocks.sm on 2026-10-31.

The silicon maximum is 3,003 MHz according to NVIDIA's GB10 documentation. I did not measure the reason for the gap. The pinned kit sets ROUND_ROWS=16, and its decode kernel processes at most 16 rows per step. Four streams with one base token plus three draft tokens each would fill 16 rows exactly. A full kernel does not permanently block another stream, but under sustained load the deferral is head-of-line blocking: repeated deferrals add queueing latency and can starve streams The arithmetic also shows that a full 16-row kernel would predict a much higher aggregate if bandwidth-bound, so the kernel is a constraint rather than the sole explanation. Only a sweep that varies clock and kernel width one at a time will tell you which candidate matters.

Prefill, prefix cache, and speculative decoding

Prefill went the other way. Across 98 sampled requests the engine sustained 39,237 tok/s of prompt processing, hundreds of times the decode rate. That is the shape the roofline predicted, because prefill is matrix-matrix work over the whole prompt and saturates the compute units rather than the memory bus. The chapter that said prefill would be fast was right. The chapter that said decode would be bandwidth-bound was right for one stream and incomplete for four.

The prefix cache is where the arithmetic gets satisfying. Of 12,804,579 prompt tokens looked up, 12,285,952 were served from cache, which is 95.9%. At the measured prefill rate, 12,285,952 divided by 39,237 gives 313 seconds of prefill work the cache removed from the sampled window. The mechanism is the one from the caching chapter. KV blocks are keyed by a hash of the token prefix, so a request that opens with a span the server has already processed skips the matmuls for that span and instead references the cached KV blocks (only the block-table entries are copied, not the block contents). The workload is consistent with the hit rate. Mean prompt length across those 98 requests was 130,659 tokens, and a long shared opening is the pattern a prefix cache is built for. I did not run a controlled test to isolate the cause.

Speculative decoding The live container's metrics report mtp_drafted_total = 62,390 and mtp_accepted_total = 42,181 for DSpark. The mechanism is a draft-and-verify loop. A small head proposes the next few tokens, the target checks all of them in a single forward pass, and every accepted token is one the target did not have to generate by itself. An acceptance rate of 67.6% is healthy, and it is also why the 16-row kernel matters. Drafts become extra rows in the verify batch, so speculation and concurrency draw on the same budget.

KV pool arithmetic and admission policy

Now the pool arithmetic, which is the part of this rig most likely to bite you. The shared KV pool holds 262,144 positions across 4 streams, set by CONTEXT=262144 in scripts/local.sh and PARALLEL=4 in the live container env. Split evenly, that is 65,536 positions per stream. Subtract the default reply budget of 32,768 tokens, the 16 round rows, and 2 positions of slack, and the per-stream admission ceiling lands at 32,750 tokens. The arithmetic is 65,536 - 32,768 - 16 - 2 = 32,750. That is what the scheduler will let a request claim when all four slots are busy. Raise the pool to the model's native 1,048,576-token window and the same arithmetic gives 229,358 tokens per stream. The computation is 1,048,576/4 = 262,144, then 262,144 - 32,768 - 16 - 2 = 229,358. The cost is 0.68 GiB of KV memory per rank.

The KV formula is positions × layers × KV heads × head dim × bytes per element. The config's 0.9 GiB per 1,048,576 positions gives 0.9 × 1024^3 / 1,048,576 = 921.6 bytes per position. The extra positions are 1,048,576 - 262,144 = 786,432. The extra bytes are 786,432 × 921.6 = 724,775,731 bytes, which is 0.675 GiB, rounded to 0.68 GiB per rank. For a much larger ceiling, that is cheap, and if you serve long prompts it is the first knob to turn.

The ceiling also exposes the gap between policy and practice. Mean prompt length in the window was 130,659 tokens, several times the 32,750 equal-split reservation. The server was not running four long requests at once. It was running fewer, and the idle slots were not holding their reservation. The scheduler tracks the free pool. Each active stream reserves MAX_TOKENS + ROUND_ROWS + 2 = 32,768 + 16 + 2 = 32,786 positions. With one active stream, free = 262,144 - 32,786 = 229,358, so a 130,659-token prompt fits. With four active streams, free per stream is 32,750. The scheduler admits a new request only if the free pool is enough for its reservation; the free pool is already net of active reservations. It does not preempt an active request to make room. If the free pool is insufficient, the request queues.

Idle slots do not hold reservations; their share is simply unused capacity. An equal-split ceiling is a worst-case admission rule rather than a description of what the machine does all day. Read it as a hard limit and you will conclude the rig is broken when it is simply underloaded.

Latency, pair stall, and the limits of the case study

Latency tells the same story from the other side. Mean time to first token was 3.46 s, mean prefill duration 3.33 s, mean decode duration 8.38 s, and mean generated tokens 607, all derived from the 98 requests counted by tensorfold:request_latency_seconds_count. First token sits just above prefill duration, which means queueing at concurrency 4 added very little. That is what you want from a scheduler. It also shows how generous the default reply budget is. 607 generated tokens against 32,768 allowed, so most requests never come close to the limit.

One number on this rig has no counterpart in a single-machine roofline. The pair has accumulated 120,000 ms of stall since start. Tensor parallelism splits each layer across the two ranks, so each layer contains two all-reduces — one after the attention output projection and one after the MLP down-projection — and when one rank falls behind, the other waits inside that collective doing nothing On a desk with two boxes and a cable, that wait is the price of the second box. It does not appear in a bandwidth calculation. The counter is cumulative and not broken down by collective or rank, so I cannot turn it into a per-layer latency or an upper bound. It is a separate term you have to measure.

This is a case study rather than a benchmark. One workload, one configuration, 98 requests, counters scraped from a live server plus two hand-run probes whose prompt shapes differ from the traffic the counters describe. The 106.2 tok/s figure came from 400-token completions; the 130,659-token mean prompt came from somewhere else entirely. These numbers cannot be compared to a published result without matching the workload, which is the argument of the methodology chapter and the reason it sits beside this one.

If I were running this rig, I would raise the pool to 1,048,576 and pay the 0.68 GiB per rank, because the 32,750-token ceiling is far above the prompts in the measured 98-request traffic, so it spends most of its life unused; the separate 130,659-token probe shows why a larger pool may be needed for other workloads. Then I would re-measure the concurrency scaling. The 1.80x is the number I trust least. It could be the 16-row kernel, it could be the clock, and it could be the pair stall, and only a sweep that varies one of those at a time will tell you which.
